How the peg pyramid works

The board is built as a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row slightly wider than the one above it. A ball that starts at the top has an equal chance of moving left or right at every peg, repeating that choice until it reaches the bottom. The final landing spot is one of the pockets arranged along the base, with one more pocket than there are rows.

Because each bounce acts like a fair split, most balls gather near the middle and only a few reach the outer pockets. In maths terms, the board behaves like a Galton board, and the results follow a binomial curve. The payout table simply inverts that curve: common landings pay less, while rare edge landings pay more.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier for a finished ball.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball passes through; more rows mean more pockets and narrower odds.
Risk level
A preset that spreads or compresses the payout table without materially changing the RTP.
House margin
The small difference between fair odds and paid odds that funds the operator.
Provably fair
A system that lets players verify the result using published hashes and seeds.
Variance
The size of the swings around the average result — the feature that risk settings really change.

No. More rows add pockets and change the shape of the distribution, so the edge payouts become larger but less likely. The overall return stays broadly the same.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

There is no mathematically superior option. Low risk keeps swings mild, while high risk concentrates value in rarer edge hits. Choose based on budget and comfort.
